Pre-flight: a check of whether a scoring function measures anything

In brief

An open-source tool checks whether the score used to rank gene edits can distinguish good designs from doing nothing. It needs only one function that returns a score. It has been used on one case, the team’s own, and no other team has used it yet. [computed]

Pre-flight applies six automated checks (D1–D6) to the scoring function built on top of a metabolic model. Two of the checks were not found automated elsewhere by the team (no survey table was kept): D1 (whether doing nothing scores as well as the best design) and D2 (whether the score separates candidates at measurable resolution). The other four automate established methods, which are identified on the Software page. The transferable principle is that the threshold a ranking must exceed is the resolution of the assay, not the numerical precision of the solver. The tool requires only an evaluate(combo, target) -> float method and is released under the MIT licence. Code: github.com/Siddhant-Chowdhary/igem-preflight (tests pass on Windows; Linux and macOS install steps are given but macOS is untested; a mirror on the iGEM GitLab is still to be made). Example output for the team’s case is on the Software page. Evidence of use by others: none yet. See also Trust.

A benchmark of when machine learning is not required

In brief

On an artificial test problem, a simple greedy search found the best answer more often than a basic learning-based search, and trying every combination stayed practical at the scale tested. A trivial baseline should therefore be tried before building machine learning. [computed]

A reinforcement-learning agent for knockout search was benchmarked against brute force, greedy and random search over 20 seeds. For 10 candidate genes and up to 3 knockouts, brute force took 52 s and was exact; greedy search found the exact best answer in 65 % of seeds with about one eighth of the evaluations; the reinforcement-learning agent found it in 10 %. With up to three simultaneous knockouts, even 50 candidate genes can be enumerated exhaustively in under two hours at 0.30 s per solve (four or five knockouts would take about a day or eight days). A trivial baseline should be tried before machine learning is built. Limits: the landscape is synthetic, the agent is a simple linear Q-learner, and the result is a benchmark of one agent on one landscape family, not a general finding. See Trust.

A worked screening life cycle assessment of a bio-based coolant

In brief

An open description is provided of how a yeast-derived coolant was compared with commercial ones, covering the choices made and their limits. The result depends on the electricity grid, and the study has not been independently reviewed. [computed]

The Life Cycle Assessment page describes how a yeast-derived ester was compared with commercial immersion coolants: functional units, system boundary, allocation choices, nine proxy datasets constructed from reaction stoichiometry where ecoinvent has none, and a use phase derived from computational fluid dynamics. The proxy datasets themselves are not shared: they are openLCA processes linked to ecoinvent background data, and whether they may be redistributed under the ecoinvent licence has not been checked. The nine materials are named on the Life Cycle Assessment page, and the per-material stoichiometry is in the team’s LCA report; neither the processes nor the report are published as a download. The limits are as informative as the method: the result depends on the grid carbon factor, several inputs are placeholders, and the study has not been critically reviewed. See Life Cycle Assessment.
