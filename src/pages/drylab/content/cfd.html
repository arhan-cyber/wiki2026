Heat-flow simulation of the coolant (CFD)

In brief

Heat-flow simulations suggest that methyl myristate, the candidate coolant, keeps a 1,200 W server below 85 °C at 0.27 m/s, whereas two reference coolants fail at 0.60 m/s and pass at 0.80 m/s, so their true minimum lies between those. The simulations have no mesh-independence check and no comparison with measurements. [computed]

  • Method. Seventeen OpenFOAM1 simulations (chtMultiRegionFoam, three-dimensional conjugate heat transfer, constant fluid properties) of one 1U server dissipating 1,200 W in a tank. Coolant enters at 25 °C. Each run was marched in time from a cold start until the server temperature stopped changing (about 5,700–6,000 s of simulated time). Runs were made on a laptop and on the IIT Bombay HPC cluster. [computed]
  • Same flow (0.42 m/s). The hottest component reached 78.1 °C with methyl myristate, compared with 87.2 °C (mineral oil) and 88.0 °C (ester-based coolant).
  • Flow needed to stay at or below 85 °C. Methyl myristate: 0.255 m/s failed and 0.27 m/s passed. Mineral oil and the ester-based coolant: 0.60 m/s failed and 0.80 m/s passed. The minimum for these two is therefore between 0.60 and 0.80 m/s; 0.80 m/s is an upper bound, not a measured minimum. Methyl myristate therefore needs between 2.2 and 3.0 times less flow, and about 11 to 26 times less pump power (6.1 W against 67.3 W at 0.60 m/s and 158.9 W at 0.80 m/s).
  • Limits. Steady-state conditions and constant fluid properties were assumed. No measurement on a physical immersion tank has been made, and the numerical set-up has not been verified (see below).

Because the numerical set-up is not documented and unverified, these results are screening estimates: they indicate the direction (methyl myristate cools better at equal flow) but the size of the differences, in particular the minimum flows, may change with mesh and model choices. Constant properties also mean that the temperature dependence of viscosity is not captured; the colour scales of the team’s plots span a coolant temperature rise of 5 K (25 to 30 °C), and the resulting error was not quantified. The properties of the ester-based reference coolant come from the manufacturer data sheet of a commercial synthetic ester dielectric fluid (MIVOLT DF7, M&I Materials, as cited in the team’s LCA report); the mineral-oil properties are team inputs whose source is not given. The data sheets are not linked from this site.

Coolant selection

In brief

A separate comparison of two candidate esters rated methyl myristate slightly higher on thermal figures of merit, but within the uncertainty, and recommended isopropyl myristate on practical grounds. The choice has not been recorded, and the simulations on this page cover methyl myristate only. [pending]

  • Thermal figures of merit [computed, data-limited]. Methyl myristate was rated about 9 % higher than isopropyl myristate, a difference within the estimated uncertainty. The selection matrix (properties, sources, uncertainty) is not reproduced on this site because the underlying analysis is not available to it; this is a gap.
  • Operability. Isopropyl myristate was recommended because methyl myristate melts at about 18.5 °C (PubChem lists 18.5 and 19 °C2), which leaves about 1.5 to 2 °C of margin at the 20 °C inlet assumed in that analysis. The CFD and the life cycle assessment assume a 25 °C inlet, which leaves about 6.5 °C; the two analyses used different inlet temperatures, and the 20 °C case was not simulated.
  • Certification. Neither ester can be certified for energised immersion until dielectric breakdown voltage, volume resistivity and pour point have been measured. Literature values of these properties for either ester have not been collected for this site. [pending]
  • Decision. The team’s choice between the two has not been recorded on this site, so the product that the strain would make is not yet fixed. The CFD and life cycle assessment therefore describe a coolant that the selection analysis does not recommend. [pending]

How this connects to the life cycle assessment

In brief

The heat-flow result sets how much pumping the coolant needs; the life cycle assessment asks whether the production footprint is repaid in use. The net result depends on the grid carbon factor and on the unmeasured minimum flow of the reference coolants. [computed, screening level]

The production footprint of methyl myristate per kilogram was far higher than that of the commercial coolants at present, whereas in use it can reduce cooling electricity. Whether the net result favours methyl myristate depends on the grid carbon factor and on where between 0.60 and 0.80 m/s the reference coolants’ minimum flow lies. The caveats and a sensitivity table are given on the Life Cycle Assessment page.
