Overview

Our project explores methyl myristate (MM) as a potential bio-based dielectric coolant for single-phase immersion cooling in AI data centres. Since physical tank experiments were not conducted, we used three-dimensional computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations in OpenFOAM to study coolant flow and heat transfer around a 1U server under a design heat load of 1,200 W. By comparing MM with conventional coolants under identical conditions, we aim to understand its cooling performance and estimate the energy requirements of the cooling system.

These results help us assess whether methyl myristate can provide effective server cooling while reducing the energy required to operate the cooling system. The estimated energy savings are based on the assumptions used in our cooling-system and life cycle assessment models.

Objective and scope

The primary objective of our CFD study was to determine the coolant flow rate required to maintain the hottest server temperature (Tmax) at or below 85 °C. By comparing the coolants at equivalent thermal performance, we could estimate the energy required for pumping and heat rejection and use these results in our LCA.

Simulation setup

We modelled a single 1U server immersed in a tank containing a dielectric coolant. The server was assigned a uniform heat load of 1,200 W, and the coolant entered the tank at 25 °C.

Coolants compared

Methyl myristate (MM)

Mineral oil

A generic refined mineral-oil-based coolant used as a conventional reference.

Ester-based coolant

A commercial synthetic ester dielectric fluid (MIVOLT DF7).

GTL-based coolant

Shell Immersion Cooling Fluid S5 X.

The study comprised 19 simulations: 17 runs covering methyl myristate, mineral oil and the ester-based coolant, as documented in Appendix A of the LCA report [27], and two additional runs for the GTL-based coolant.

Numerics and convergence check

A simulation was considered complete only after the server temperature stabilised and the coolant removed approximately the full 1,200 W heat load. The following checks were used to assess numerical convergence and consistency.

  • Steady state: Maximum server temperature changed by less than 0.02 K over 200 s, followed by a 1,000 s run after averaging stabilised [1].
  • Energy balance: Heat removal was calculated using mass flow rate, specific heat capacity and bulk inlet–outlet temperature difference. Converged cases balanced within ±2% of the 1,200 W heat load.
  • Faster simulations: Server heat capacity was reduced tenfold to shorten warm-up time. Warm starts from neighbouring converged cases improved computational efficiency.
  • Pressure-drop consistency: At 0.80 m/s, pressure drops were 234 Pa for mineral oil, ~242 Pa for MM and 259 Pa for the ester-based coolant, consistent with inertia-dominated losses.

Coolant properties

The thermophysical properties used in our CFD simulations were compiled from published literature and manufacturer datasheets. All properties were treated as constant at 25 °C (298.15 K) to ensure a consistent comparison between coolants.

Viscosity is a key factor in this comparison. Methyl myristate (MM) has a lower dynamic viscosity than the other coolants, allowing it to flow more easily and reducing the pumping requirements at its operating point.

Simulation results

We evaluated the coolants using two approaches: at a fixed inlet flow rate to compare cooling performance under identical conditions, and at steady state under the 85 °C server temperature limit to identify the minimum compliant flow rate. The results below compare their thermal performance, pressure drop and energy requirements.

Conclusion

Across both comparisons, methyl myristate (MM) demonstrates the strongest cooling performance among the tested coolants. At an identical inlet velocity of 0.42 m/s, it keeps the maximum server temperature at 78.1 °C, around 9–10 °C lower than mineral oil and the ester-based coolant. When the server temperature is constrained to 85 °C, MM meets the limit at just 0.27 m/s, compared with 0.80 m/s for both coolants. This lower flow requirement reduces estimated pump power by approximately 26 times, highlighting MM's potential to improve cooling efficiency and reduce energy consumption in AI data centres.

Visualisations

The 3-D renders show the same result as the numbers: the server surface is green-yellow (about 55–65 °C) in MM and orange-red in mineral oil and the ester-based coolant. All renders and videos were made with ParaView 5.10 and FFmpeg [16–18, 22].

In the flow tubes of Figure 3, coolant enters cold (dark) at the inlet, sweeps past the end of the server, warms along its hot faces (orange to yellow) and leaves at the outlet. For readability, the 3-D views show the server's long axis upright; gravity acts along the model's z axis (see the axis triad). All visuals use the same colour scales: server temperature 25–90 °C (Turbo), coolant temperature 25–30 °C (Inferno), coolant speed 0–0.45 m/s (Viridis).

The visualisations illustrate how coolant flow and heat distribution influence server temperature. Together with the numerical results, they support methyl myristate's potential to maintain safe operating temperatures at lower flow rates under the simulated conditions.

Model and solver

The model uses a full 3-D conjugate heat-transfer approach, coupling heat conduction within the server with heat transfer to the surrounding coolant [1–3]. A uniform heat source represents the server's thermal load, while the coolant flow transports heat away from its surface. This allows us to compare the temperature distribution and cooling performance of different coolants under the same operating conditions.

Limitations and assumptions

To ensure a fair comparison, all coolants were simulated using the same geometry, mesh and numerical settings. However, the results are subject to the following assumptions and limitations, which should be considered when interpreting the findings.

  1. Simplified setup. We modelled the server as a solid block with uniform heat generation of 1,200 W, rather than realistic server components. Only one server was modelled, so the predicted flow requirements may differ from those in a full-scale tank.
  2. Constant properties. Coolant properties were held constant at 25 °C, although they vary with temperature in practice.
  3. Numerical instability. The MM simulation at 0.80 m/s did not converge, although its pressure drop did. The design cases at 0.27–0.42 m/s converged successfully.
  4. Validation. Mesh-independence testing and experimental validation have not yet been completed.

Despite these limitations, using consistent geometry and simulation settings provides a common basis for comparing coolant performance.

Energy implications for the life cycle analysis

CFD provides the cooling performance and flow requirements for each coolant, which are then used to estimate pumping power and annual cooling energy consumption. These estimates serve as inputs to the Life Cycle Assessment (LCA), allowing us to evaluate how differences in cooling performance translate into energy use and potential environmental impacts over time. The LCA methodology and results are discussed in the LCA tab.
