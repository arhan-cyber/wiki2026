Screening-level study, not critically reviewed. The study has not undergone an ISO 14044 critical review1 and should not be used for public comparative claims. Several inputs are placeholders (listed below, with their effect). The result depends on the grid carbon factor and on the unmeasured minimum flow of the reference coolants, as shown in the results section. It should be read as a method and a first estimate, not as a verdict. Status as of 17 October 2026.

Goal and scope

In brief

A screening life cycle assessment compared the candidate bio-based coolant, methyl myristate, with two commercial coolant families. Its aim is to show which production steps matter and what would be needed to match the existing products. It is a first estimate, not a verdict. [computed]

Air cooling is approaching its limits as data-centre power density rises, and single-phase immersion cooling is an alternative. The incumbent dielectric fluids each carry a liability: fluorinated fluids (PFAS) face phase-out under the proposed EU universal PFAS restriction,2 whereas ester-based and mineral-oil-based coolants are made largely from fossil feedstocks. The team is engineering Saccharomyces cerevisiae to produce methyl myristate (MM), a C14 fatty acid methyl ester, as a bio-based, biodegradable, halogen-free dielectric fluid. This study assesses how MM compares with the incumbents.

Objectives. (1) To compare the impacts of MM with those of two commercial coolant families, ester-based and mineral-oil-based, using the same database, method and process detail. (2) To identify the fermentation and downstream steps that drive the impacts of MM, in order to guide strain and process design. (3) To test the productivity, energy integration and renewable energy that MM requires to match the incumbents. Isopropyl myristate, named in an earlier scope draft, has not been modelled.

Functional units, system boundary and methods

In brief

Two measures were used: one kilogram of coolant, and cooling one 1,200 W server for a year. The second matters because the fluids need different pumping power. The study follows the ISO life cycle assessment standards and uses ecoinvent data. [computed]

Functional units. FU-1: 1 kg of finished coolant at the plant gate (cradle-to-gate). FU-2: cooling of one 1,200 W server for one year (8,760 h), with the hottest component at or below 85 °C and coolant supply at 25 °C (the same inlet temperature as the CFD runs), covering production plus use-phase cooling electricity. Per-kg results alone ignore that the fluids require different flow rates and pumping energy to cool the same server. The IT electricity of the server (10,512 kWh yr⁻¹) is identical for every coolant and was excluded.

Foreground data. Most MM data originate from the team’s process flow design: a 100 m³ reactor giving 45.6 kg of MM (98.5 % purity) per batch, sugarcane molasses followed by AFEX-pretreated corn stover hydrolysate, and in-situ transesterification with sulfuric acid and methanol. Where ecoinvent has no dataset (nine materials, including linear alpha-olefin, boron trifluoride, trimethylolpropane, p-toluenesulfonic acid, a PFAS additive and corn stover), a proxy process was built from the industrial reaction stoichiometry. Use-phase data were taken from 17 OpenFOAM10 CFD simulations of an immersion tank run by the team (listed with their set-up and limits on the CFD page). The batch basis implies an overall methyl myristate concentration of 0.46 g per litre of reactor volume (45.6 kg in 100 m³) and a yield of 0.83 % of the dry cell mass (5,498 kg per batch); see the explanation of the per-kilogram value below.

Allocation. Without allocation, the entire burden falls on MM (3,830 kg CO₂-eq kg⁻¹); economic allocation gives 632 and mass allocation about 394 kg CO₂-eq kg⁻¹. Economic allocation was retained because it is the stricter choice for MM. The 16.5 % factor rests on a price ratio of about 1.7 per kilogram between the C14-rich distillate and the heavy ester fraction; the market prices behind that ratio have no cited source yet. The mass share of MM is 10.3 % in the report’s allocation table (47.54 kg against 415.06 kg per batch) and 9.9 % on a per-kilogram basis (1 in 1 + 9.1); the two bases were not reconciled, and both give about 10 % and about 394 kg CO₂-eq kg⁻¹.

PFAS. MM, the ester-based coolant and both mineral-oil-based coolants contain no PFAS. A clearly labelled hypothetical comparator with a fluorinated additive was modelled for illustration only. Midpoint methods score emissions but not persistence or accumulation, which are the reasons for the restriction of PFAS, and PFAS content is therefore reported as a separate hazard indicator.

Impact assessment results

In brief

Making methyl myristate emits far more carbon per kilogram than the commercial coolants, but it needs much less pumping power in use. Whether the total is lower depends on how carbon-intensive the local electricity is and on where the reference coolants’ minimum flow lies: on the inputs used here it is 17 % lower on one grid factor and 14 % higher on another, and the 17 % can turn into a 11 % increase if the reference coolant’s true minimum flow is at the low end of its bracket. These figures use placeholder inputs (20 L fill, 5-year life) and a derived grid factor. [computed]

Per kilogram, MM exceeded the commercial coolants by a large margin. Process energy (about 95 % of the footprint, led by methanol recovery) is the hotspot. Reduction of methanol to 20 mL g⁻¹, heat integration and gentler cell disruption lower the value to about 270; higher lipid and C14 content per cell would lower it further. The per-kg gap is probably overstated because MM data are scaled from laboratory and design calculations whereas commercial data originate from mature plants.

Why the per-kilogram value is so high. The value of 449 kg CO₂-eq kg⁻¹ is about 85 times that of the ester-based coolants and follows from the batch design, not from a fixed property of the chemistry. One 100 m³ batch delivers 45.6 kg of 98.5 % methyl myristate while handling 5,498 kg of dry cells. The fatty acid content was set to 8 % of dry cell mass, and the profile was borrowed from the Yarrowia I1220W result (C14 about 11.6 % of fatty acids), giving roughly 51 kg of C14 per batch before esterification and distillation losses; 45.6 kg is about 0.83 % of the dry cell mass (derived here from the report’s inputs). Methanol recovery dominates the energy. The report itself estimates that more lipid and more C14 per cell would take the value towards about 6. The value is therefore a consequence of low assumed lipid and C14 content at laboratory-derived scale, not a prediction for a real strain; it is not linked to the flux balance analysis (see below).

Per server-year: dependence on the grid factor [computed]

MM kept the server below 85 °C at 0.27 m s⁻¹, whereas the ester-based coolant failed at 0.60 and passed at 0.80 m s⁻¹, so its true minimum is between those values; 0.80 m s⁻¹ is an upper bound. Taking the upper bound, MM needed about one third of the flow and 26 times less pump power (6.1 against 158.9 W); at the lower end of the bracket the ratios would be 2.2 times and 11 times. The saving of about 1,600 kWh of cooling electricity per year is the difference between the chiller-case electricity at 0.80 m s⁻¹ for the ester-based coolant (3,773 kWh yr⁻¹, pump plus air-cooled chiller at COP 5, heat rejected including pump heat) and for MM at 0.27 m s⁻¹ (2,167 kWh yr⁻¹): 3,773 − 2,167 = 1,606 kWh. It is therefore the largest value the bracket allows; with the failing 0.60 m s⁻¹ run (2,810 kWh yr⁻¹) as the other end, the saving would be at least about 640 kWh. The value of the saving depends on the carbon intensity of the electricity (Table 3).

Other indicators [computed]

In the team’s report MM was the best of the three coolants on 8 of 9 reported metrics; the exception was production per kg (the nine-metric table is not reproduced here). The hottest server component at equal flow (0.42 m s⁻¹) reached 78.1 °C with MM against 87.2 °C (mineral oil) and 88.0 °C (ester-based). Partial power usage effectiveness (pPUE, cooling overhead only) was 1.035 for MM at 0.27 m s⁻¹ with free cooling (cooling tower and fans at 3 % of the heat rejected, 7 K approach to wet-bulb), against 1.166 for the reference coolants at 0.80 m s⁻¹, and 1.206 with a chiller; the free-cooling assumptions come from the team’s report and cite engineering standards (ASHRAE) that are not listed in this page’s references. About 93 % of the carbon in MM (C₁₅H₃₀O₂) is biogenic: 14 of 15 carbon atoms come from the fermentation-derived acid, and the methanol-derived methyl carbon is fossil in the base case (derived here from the formula). Readily biodegradable is a statement based on a study of biodiesel methyl esters,9 not of methyl myristate itself. End-of-life fossil CO₂ is lower than that of mineral-oil-based coolants because less of the carbon is fossil.

Coolant selection: a separate analysis favours isopropyl myristate

In brief

A separate comparison recommended a related ester, isopropyl myristate, mainly because methyl myristate melts at about 18.5 °C, which leaves little margin at the 20 °C inlet assumed in that comparison (this assessment and the CFD use a 25 °C supply). The team has not recorded a decision, and this assessment covers methyl myristate only. [pending]

A coolant-selection comparison of methyl myristate and isopropyl myristate (IPM) rated MM about 9 % higher on thermal figures of merit, a margin within the uncertainty of the estimated heat capacity and conductivity (the selection matrix itself is not available to this site). It nevertheless recommended IPM on operability grounds: MM melts at about 18.5 °C,7 which leaves about 1.5 to 2 °C of margin at the 20 °C inlet assumed in that analysis, whereas IPM melts at about 3 °C.8 The CFD and this assessment assume a 25 °C supply, which leaves about 6.5 °C for MM; the 20 °C case was not simulated. Cold-start trace heating is not modelled. Neither ester can be certified for energised immersion until breakdown voltage, volume resistivity, dielectric loss and pour point have been measured. This life cycle assessment models MM only. [pending] The team’s decision between the two esters.

Consistency with the metabolic model

In brief

The assessment does not yet match the metabolic model in three ways: it borrows a fatty-acid profile from another yeast, assumes a different sugar, and models the ester form while the metabolic model predicts the free acid. These must be reconciled. [pending]

The output of the yeast strain enters the life cycle assessment as MM. Three links are not yet consistent with the metabolic model: (1) the assessment borrows a fatty-acid profile from the Yarrowia I1220W result, which is the assumption that the Model identifies as unsupported in S. cerevisiae; (2) it assumes xylose use from corn-stover hydrolysate, whereas the flux balance analysis (FBA) optimises free C14:0 on glucose; (3) the FBA predicts free myristate, whereas the life cycle assessment models methyl myristate, so the esterification step must be stated. [pending] The borrowed profile should be replaced with the lipid content and C14 share measured for the team strain.

Limitations and open items

In brief

Several inputs are placeholders or known to be wrong, packaging, transport and end of life are not modelled, and no independent review has been done. The results are therefore a screening estimate. [pending]

The list below comprises the open items of the report plus items found in review, with what each does to the result where that can be said.

  • The exact ecoinvent grid factor should be read from a 1 kWh openLCA run (currently about 1.6, derived from the hotspot split) and used as the base case. Effect: the sign of the comparison already changes between 1.6 and 0.716 (Table 3).
  • Mineral oil should be exported from openLCA and the 1.5 kg kg⁻¹ placeholder replaced. Effect: about 5 kg CO₂-eq per server-year against about 2,700 in use, so negligible per server-year but not per kilogram.
  • The in-vivo methylation scenario (in which 20 % of the methylation is assumed to occur in the cell) is not reported on this page, because its current openLCA result (629 kg CO₂-eq kg⁻¹ against about 575 expected) is inconsistent and awaits a rerun after the process links are checked.
  • The PFAS proxy in the ester-based coolant should be set to zero and the export repeated (the 14 October export still included it; effect under 0.01 kg CO₂-eq kg⁻¹, negligible).
  • The tank fill should be taken from the simulation geometry (20 L and a 5-year life are placeholders). Effect: the production part of the MM result scales with fill divided by life; break-even in 5 years needs a fill below about 11 L (baseline) or 15 L (optimised).
  • The optimised process (DC-2, VD-1, HR-1, BC-1 changes) should be linked and steps 1–3 rerun with fossil methanol.
  • The commercial coolants should be run at 0.65 and 0.70 m s⁻¹ to bracket their true minimum flow. Effect: large; the optimised-MM result moves from −17 % to about +11 % on the 1.6 grid across the bracket (Table 3b).
  • The market prices behind the 16.5 % allocation should be cited, and the mass-allocation share resolved (10.3 % and 9.9 % on two bases). Effect: mass allocation lowers the MM value to about 394 kg CO₂-eq kg⁻¹, still about 75 times the ester-based coolants.
  • A sensitivity (tornado) analysis and an uncertainty (Monte Carlo) analysis should be added; packaging, transport and end of life modelled; and isopropyl myristate modelled. Table 3b is a first, partial sensitivity only.
  • An ISO 14044 critical review should be obtained if comparative claims are to be made. The openLCA exports and the team report are not published as downloads.
