Overview

Our CFD tab showed that methyl myristate (MM) keeps a 1,200 W server below 85 °C with about a third of the coolant flow that commercial coolants need. But a coolant is only greener if its whole life is greener, including the factory that makes it. So we ran a life cycle assessment (LCA): we followed MM and two commercial coolant families, ester-based and mineral-oil-based, from raw materials through manufacturing to a year of cooling a server, and added up the environmental impact at every step.

The question

Making MM costs more CO₂ than making today's coolants. Does the electricity it saves in the data centre make up for it?

What is a Life Cycle Assessment?

An LCA is the standard way to answer "which product is better for the planet?" fairly. Three ideas are all you need:

1. A carbon receipt for a product's whole life

Every kilogram of molasses, litre of methanol and kilowatt-hour of electricity used to make and run the coolant becomes a line item with an environmental cost, from cradle (raw materials) to use.

2. Establish a basis

Products are only comparable on the same basis, called the functional unit. We establish two: 1 kg of coolant (how clean is production?) and cooling one 1.2 kW server for a year (what does a data centre actually pay?).

3. A standard, four-step method

ISO 14040/14044 set the steps: goal and scope, inventory, impact assessment, interpretation [1, 2]. Impacts go beyond CO₂: we scored 16 categories, from water use to toxicity [6].

Why our coolant is superior in use

It flows easily

MM's viscosity is 0.0051 Pa·s, about a third of the ester-based (0.0150) and mineral oil (0.0121) values used on the CFD tab, so it carries heat away at low flow.

It needs a third of the flow

MM meets 85 °C at 0.27 m/s; the others need 0.80 m/s. Pump power rises with the cube of flow: 6.1 W vs 158.9 W.

It cuts cooling electricity

2,167 vs 3,773 kWh/yr with a chiller (−43%); 370 vs 1,749 kWh/yr with free cooling (−79%). With free cooling, the cooling overhead is 3.5% vs 16.6% of the server power.

Its carbon is cleaner

93% biogenic carbon (ester 82%, mineral oil 0%), 17× less fossil CO₂ if burned at end of life, no PFAS, and readily biodegradable.

Does it hold on a cleaner grid?

MM's advantage is electricity it does not use, so the advantage shrinks when that electricity is cleaner. Our base case uses the ecoinvent India grid (~1.6 kg CO₂-eq/kWh) for both production and use; as a sensitivity we used the Central Electricity Authority's grid-average factor (0.716) [31].

Conclusion

Per kilogram, MM is still expensive to make. Per server, it is the greener coolant: 17% less CO₂ a year today, 28% after our next process steps, and it pays back its production footprint in under 3 years.

Our bioprocess model: process flow diagram (PFD) calculations

No MM factory exists yet, so we designed one on paper. We drew the full process flow diagram for one 100 m³ batch and calculated a mass and energy balance for every unit in it. Those numbers became the MM inventory in openLCA.

Process flow diagram of our MM plant

1. Grow

Engineered yeast grows on molasses and corn-stover hydrolysate in an aerated bubble-column fermenter (BC-1), fed by air compressors.

2. Harvest and dry

A centrifuge (SDC-1) and vacuum dryer (VD-1) recover 5,498 kg of dry cells per batch.

3. Convert

In-situ transesterification in HR-1: cells are broken and their fatty acids turned into methyl esters with methanol and 0.2 M H₂SO₄ [13–16].

4. Purify

Decanter, Nutsche filter and flash vessels, then DC-2 recovers methanol for recycle and DC-1 distils 45.6 kg of 98.5% MM plus a heavy FAME co-product.

How the PFD calculations work

  1. Mass balance per unit: each stream (feeds, cells, methanol, acid, water, CO₂, product, waste) is tracked through every unit from stoichiometry, yields and recovery fractions [12–16].
  2. Energy per unit: sensible heating, evaporation (latent heat) and vacuum for the columns and dryer [20]; aeration, mixing, homogenisation and pumping power for the electrical units; fermentation heat removal from oxygen uptake [19].
  3. Utilities: process cooling by chiller (COP 4.5) and cooling tower (0.03 kWh per kWh rejected, 1.6 kg water per kWh) [32].
  4. Into openLCA: each unit became an openLCA process; its steam, electricity and chemicals were linked to ecoinvent datasets (Indian first, then rest-of-world, then global).
  5. Allocation: DC-1 makes MM and heavy FAME, so its burden is split by economic value: 16.5% to MM.

Modelling the commercial coolants with openLCA and ecoinvent

Commercial coolant makers do not publish their inventories, so we rebuilt each factory from its published industrial route, the same way we built ours, then let openLCA and ecoinvent supply the upstream chain (ethylene, coconut oil, electricity, steam, transport).

  1. Route from industrial practice: BF₃-catalysed oligomerisation of 1-decene from ethylene for the synthetic mineral-oil grade [24, 26]; acid-catalysed esterification of trimethylolpropane with C8–C10 acids for the ester [25, 27].
  2. Mass balance from stoichiometry: feeds, recycles, co-products and wastes from reaction stoichiometry and typical conversions.
  3. Energy per unit operation: heating, vaporisation, vacuum and pumping duties, step by step [20].
  4. Proxies for missing chemicals: nine materials with no ecoinvent dataset (linear alpha-olefin, BF₃, trimethylolpropane, p-toluenesulfonic acid, a PFAS additive, corn stover and others) were built from their reaction stoichiometry, a published method for filling inventory gaps [28, 29, 38].
  5. Checked against a real plant: for the ester we swapped our bottom-up energy (2.00 MJ + 0.107 kWh/kg) for a measured commercial plant (5.32 MJ + 0.63 kWh/kg) [23]. Both results (5.17 and 5.25) sit inside the published 3.07–5.87 kg CO₂-eq/kg range for ester base oils [23].

Equipment, efficiencies and heat integration in the commercial models

  • Same utilities as MM: every heating duty draws on the same ecoinvent steam dataset and every kWh on the same Indian grid, so no coolant gets a cleaner energy supply.
  • Duties step by step: reactor heating, distillation reboilers, vacuum stripping, drying and pumping were calculated per kg from heat capacities, latent heats and typical industrial conditions [20]. In the synthetic grade, the two distillation columns take 1.4 of the 2.73 MJ per kg.
  • No extra heat-integration credit: we did not add recompression or heat pumps to the commercial bottom-up models.
  • Real plants set the reported number: a bottom-up model assumes ideal equipment, so for the ester we report a measured commercial plant instead (5.32 MJ + 0.63 kWh per kg [23]), which already contains that plant's real equipment efficiencies and heat recovery. It uses 2.7× the heat of our bottom-up model, a reminder that ideal calculations underestimate real plants.

Fair operating point

We use the lowest flow actually simulated that passes. MM fails at 0.25 m/s, so 0.27 m/s is slightly conservative for MM.

Honest about the bracket

The commercial coolants fail at 0.60 and pass at 0.80 m/s. Even at 0.70 m/s their pump would draw ~106 W, still 17× MM's 6.1 W. Runs at 0.65 and 0.70 m/s are planned.

More free-cooling hours

With a 7 K cooling-tower approach, MM's 28 °C supply limit allows free cooling up to ~21 °C wet-bulb, against ~19.5 °C for the others.

Same loop for everyone

Plate heat exchanger, pipes and filter: 20 kPa at 34.4 L/min, scaling with flow². This is conservative for low-viscosity MM.

From CFD to kilowatt-hours

We turned each operating point into electricity and CO₂ with four short equations, applied identically to every coolant [32, 33]:

Pump power: P = 23.0 W × (v ÷ 0.42 m/s)³

(20 kPa loop + tank pressure drop at 34.4 L/min, wire-to-water efficiency 0.5; pressure drop ∝ flow², so power ∝ flow³)

Cooling electricity, chiller: E = 8,760 h × [ (1,200 W + P) ÷ COP 5 + P ]

Cooling electricity, free cooling: E = 8,760 h × [ 0.03 × (1,200 W + P) + P ]

CO₂ per server-year: (20 L × density ÷ 5 yr) × production footprint + E × grid factor

How we brought PFAS into the LCA

Some immersion coolants are fluorinated, and PFAS ("forever chemicals") are now being restricted in Europe [8]. Standard LCA methods miss most of their harm, because they score how toxic an emission is, not how long it lasts.

How we found the data: literature review

We reviewed the PFAS literature for three things: why PFAS in fluids are being restricted (the ECHA restriction report [8]) and how they are classified [30]; which compound to track, since C6 fluorotelomer additives break down to the persistent PFHxA [7, 30]; and published toxicity factors for it, which we took from the PFAS-adapted USEtox framework of Holmquist et al. [7]. We then checked ecoinvent 3.9.1 and EF 3.1 and found no dataset for a fluorinated coolant additive and no PFHxA factor, so we built both ourselves in four steps:

1. Extend the method

We copied EF 3.1 in openLCA and added PFHxA toxicity factors (200 CTUe/kg freshwater ecotoxicity; 6×10⁻⁶ CTUh/kg human toxicity) from the PFAS-adapted USEtox framework [7].

2. Build a PFAS proxy

No fluorinated additive exists in ecoinvent, so we built one from fluorine chemistry stoichiometry [29, 30].

3. Model a hypothetical

A clearly labelled ester with 1 g/kg fluorinated additive: climate ~5.26 kg CO₂-eq/kg, almost the same as without it.

4. Report hazard separately

Since climate scores barely move, PFAS content is reported as its own hazard indicator. MM and both commercial families contain 0 g PFAS.

The lesson: a carbon number alone would call a fluorinated coolant "almost as green". Persistence is why PFAS matter, so we flag it separately instead of hiding it inside a CO₂ total.

Limitations and Assumptions

Every coolant used the same database, method and boundary, so the comparison is fair. The absolute numbers carry these caveats:

Screening study

No ISO 14044 critical review yet, so these results are not a basis for public comparative claims. Packaging, transport and end of life are not yet modelled.

Placeholders

20 L fill and 5-year life; refined mineral oil at ~1.5 kg CO₂-eq/kg; the ecoinvent grid factor (~1.6) is derived and still to be read from a 1 kWh run.

Grid matters

On the cleaner CEA grid factor (0.716), the optimised process is +14% vs the ester; only the steps 1–3 process stays ahead (−9%).

Lab to plant

MM is scaled from lab and design data with a borrowed fatty-acid profile. MM melts at ~18 °C [35]; cold-start heating is not modelled.

Despite these limits, one result is robust: in use, MM needs far less cooling energy, and the faster we cut its production footprint, the bigger and safer its advantage.

What this means for our project

The LCA tells the wet lab exactly where to aim: more lipid and more C14 per cell means more MM per batch, which shrinks every per-kg number on this page. It tells the process design to cut methanol first. And it tells data centres that a bio-based coolant can already beat today's fluids over a server's life.
