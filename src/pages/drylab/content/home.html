Welcome to the Dry Lab. This is the computational half of our project: the modelling behind a yeast that could make myristic acid for a bio-based data-centre coolant. The work starts from one residue of fatty acid synthase and ends at a coolant’s life cycle, and the pages follow that order. The wet lab built the V1254W construct that these models point to; Integration below shows how the two labs fit together. Start with the Model overview, then read CFD for the cooling performance and LCA for the footprint. The Engineering page lists every cycle we ran and what each changed. Each section opens on a short summary and expands on click.

Summary. The IIT Bombay iGEM 2026 dry-lab team modelled the engineering of Saccharomyces cerevisiae to produce a C14:0-rich fatty-acid product (myristic acid) that can be esterified to methyl myristate, a bio-based immersion coolant for data centres. Five linked modelling steps and two downstream analyses (CFD and LCA) were built, from a single residue of fatty acid synthase to a coolant life cycle assessment. Sequence alignment identified V1254 as the residue equivalent to the Yarrowia chain-length residue, and that is the mutation the wet lab built. In the metabolic model, an early-release reaction increased C14 export by 2.6 %, limited by NADPH; none of the 175 knockout sets of up to three of ten named genes exceeded the wild type, so effort went into the single mutation. In use, methyl myristate keeps a 1,200 W server below 85 °C at about a third of the flow of commercial coolants and emits 17 % less CO₂-eq per server-year.

Integration with the wet lab

In brief

Dry lab and wet lab work through the same five design cycles, A to E. Each model feeds a wet-lab step, and the measured C14 profile closes the loop back to the models and the life cycle assessment.

The two labs share one project question: can a single FAS2 mutation shift yeast fatty acids toward C14? The models chose the site and set the expectations; the wet lab built the construct and measures the result. Our structure-validation rounds are the Design and Test detail of cycles A and B (see Engineering).

Overview of the modelling work

In brief

One question was asked at several scales, from a single amino acid to a coolant’s life cycle, and each step passed a result to the next. The key assumption, that one mutation can make yeast release the 14-carbon fatty acid early, is the one the wet-lab construct tests.

The modelling addressed one question at several scales and then examined whether the answer was reliable and worth acting upon (five modelling steps, with CFD and LCA grouped as the sixth, ‘Process’):

  1. Sequence. Which residue of yeast fatty acid synthase (FAS) controls chain length?
  2. FAS interaction. Does the binding pocket of the enzyme accommodate a C14 chain, and which residues should remain unaltered? (The pose descriptions depend on the setup, so the residue exclusion list is the result used.)
  3. Bridge. Can a genome-scale metabolic model represent that mutation?
  4. Metabolism. What can the whole cell reach, at what cost to growth, and by which route?
  5. Trust. Does the scoring of candidate designs measure anything, and is a learned search better than a simple one?
  6. Process. Is the product suitable for its intended use, and how does it compare with other coolants?

Key points

In brief

The models chose the V1254W route, predicted a modest C14 gain limited by NADPH, and showed that methyl myristate is the better coolant in use. The wet-lab construct tests the central assumption.

  1. The models selected one mutation. In the metabolic model none of the 175 knockout sets of up to three of ten named genes exceeded wild-type C14:0 production (with and without the early-release reaction), and the pre-flight checks showed the scoring setup could not support a ranked list. Effort went into the V1254W route. These results describe one model under stated conditions, not a strain.
  2. The mutation route has a precedent and an experimental test. The proposal that a FAS2 point mutation can release C14 in S. cerevisiae rests on a Yarrowia lipolytica result1, and the wet-lab V1254W construct is its test in S. cerevisiae. All downstream numbers are conditional on it.
  3. Methyl myristate wins in use and loses on production. The screening life cycle assessment gives 5,012 kg CO₂-eq per server-year against 6,071 for an ester-based coolant (−17 %), with 6.1 W against 158.9 W of pump power, while making 1 kg emits 449 kg CO₂-eq against about 5. See Life Cycle Assessment.
