Step 5. Reliability of the strain-design ranking

In brief

The team’s own method for ranking gene deletions could not tell the best strain from the unedited one, so an automated checking tool withheld any recommendations. A simple benchmark also showed that a simple search method does nearly as well as exhaustive search, while a learning-based method did worse.

This step examines whether the ranking of gene knockouts produced by the scoring function carries information that could justify an experiment. Three analyses are reported: a failure of the scoring function against its own checks, a comparison of distance measures, and a benchmark of search methods.

Failure of the scoring function against its checks

In brief

The best-ranked knockout scored only 0.010 % better than the unedited strain, far less than the 1–5 % variation expected between repeated measurements. The ranking therefore could not be acted on.

The pipeline ranked gene knockouts by the agreement (cosine similarity, with a growth penalty) between the predicted fatty-acid profile and a target profile (C14:0 0.6, C16:0 0.2, C18:0 0.1, C18:1 0.1). The candidate genes were ten named genes: POX1, PXA1, PXA2, FAA1, FAA4, DGA1, LRO1, ARE1, ARE2 and OLE1 (the team’s choice of genes in fatty-acid degradation, activation, storage and desaturation; the choice was not tested against alternatives), giving 10 + 45 + 120 = 175 combinations of up to three knockouts. Exhaustive evaluation of all 175 returned FAA1 + FAA4 as the highest-scoring set (score 0.202529). The unedited strain scored 0.202509, a difference of 0.010 %. Note that this objective (profile match) differs from the objective of the Metabolism page (maximum C14 export at a growth floor), where FAA1 + FAA4 is 2 % below wild type; the two statements describe different quantities and do not contradict each other. The composition of fatty acids measured by gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC-MS) is assumed to vary by roughly 1–5 % between biological replicates (an assumption without a cited source or team replicate data), so an experiment could not be expected to separate the two strains if that assumption holds. The search was exact and the arithmetic correct; the scoring function did not discriminate between candidates, and no error message indicated this.

Pre-flight checks D1–D6

In brief

An automated tool called pre-flight runs six checks on a scoring function. Three failed and three gave warnings for the team’s setup, so the tool issued no strain proposals.

The pre-flight tool (Software page) applies six automated checks to a scoring function; each returns PASS, WARN or FAIL with an explanation (Table 1). The thresholds are tied to assay resolution rather than to solver tolerance. A first version compared score differences with solver tolerance (about one part in a million) and passed the pipeline described above.

Comparison of loss functions

In brief

Ten ways of measuring how far a predicted fatty-acid mixture is from a target were compared. A simple one, total variation, is the recommended default, and designs closer than about 0.014 cannot be told apart at 3 % measurement noise. The target used was illustrative.

Ten distance measures for fatty-acid profiles were compared: total variation, L2, cosine, Jensen–Shannon, Hellinger, Kullback–Leibler (KL), Bray–Curtis, Aitchison, and one- and two-dimensional Earth Mover’s distance. Every metric except cosine and Aitchison resolved only about 4–8 % relative differences at 3 % noise (Table 1b). Here ‘resolved’ means the 95th percentile of the change in the distance between target and candidate when the candidate profile is re-measured with 3 % noise, divided by the typical distance. Total variation is the recommended default (a true metric between 0 and 1; for a pure-C14 target it equals one minus the C14:0 fraction). The runner-up is the two-dimensional chain-aware Earth Mover’s distance, the only candidate for which C14:0 is closer to C16:0 than to C18:1. Designs whose total-variation losses differ by less than about 0.014 are tied at 3 % noise. The profile target and the yeast-like background used here are illustrative; the GC-MS noise level of 1–5 % is itself an assumption.

Cost-effectiveness of learned search relative to brute force and greedy search

In brief

On an artificial test problem, a simple greedy search found the best answer 65 % of the time, while a basic reinforcement-learning method found it 10 % of the time and needed far more evaluations. This does not show that the learning approach cannot work.

[computed, synthetic landscape] A reinforcement-learning (RL) agent for knockout search was benchmarked over 20 random seeds against brute force and greedy hill-climbing (Table 2, Figure 2). The result concerns one linear Q-learning agent on one family of synthetic landscapes (Gaussian main effects of each gene on four products, a growth cost per gene, sparse pairwise interactions, score = cosine similarity to a target times a growth penalty). It does not show that learned search is unnecessary in general.

Random search on the RL agent’s budget (1,204 evaluations) matched greedy search (99.5–100 % of the exact optimum at 10 to 50 genes, one seed). This is uninformative at 10 genes, where the budget is seven times the size of the whole space (175 combinations); at 30 to 50 genes it suggests many near-optimal solutions. An earlier RL run on a four-product profile of the real model reported only 3 distinct flux vectors in 122 evaluations; that observation was not independently verified and is not used as evidence here.

Cost of enumeration

In brief

With up to three simultaneous knockouts, trying every combination stays practical for up to about 50 candidate genes at the measured speed of 0.30 s per calculation, and greedy search was much faster and came close to the best answer. With four or five knockouts the same 50 genes would take about 21 hours or 8 days. In this range a learned search was not needed.

At 0.30 s per flux balance analysis (FBA) solve (measured on the team’s evaluator), exact search over up to three simultaneous knockouts of 10 candidate genes takes 52 s, 20 genes 7 min, 30 genes 23 min, 40 genes 54 min and 50 genes 1.7 h. Greedy search took 6–35 s throughout and reached 99.5–100 % of the exact optimum (single seed; indicative only). In this range a learned search was not required. The combination counts (175; 1,350; 4,525; 10,700; 20,875) multiplied by 0.30 s reproduce these times. For four knockouts at 50 genes there are 251,175 combinations (21 h), and for five 2,369,935 (8 days).
