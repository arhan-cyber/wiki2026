Step 3. Representation of the mutation in a metabolic model

In brief

The genome-scale yeast model had no way to represent a fatty acid synthase that releases 14-carbon chains early, so one was added. It raised the model’s maximum myristic acid output by only 2.6 %, because the limiting resource is a cofactor (NADPH), not the carbon supply.

Question. Can Yeast91 represent early C14 release from fatty acid synthase (FAS)?

Finding. Yeast9 represents FAS as reactions that terminate at C16:0-CoA and C18:0-CoA and contains no reaction for early C14 release. The model therefore could not represent the intended intervention, and an earlier workaround added a myristate exchange reaction by hand. A documented early-release reaction (fbareduce_FAS_C14_release) was added and the analysis was repeated.

The reaction. All species are cytosolic; irreversible (bounds 0 to 1000 mmol gDW⁻¹ h⁻¹); mass- and charge-balanced at build time; gene rule FAS1 and FAS2 (YKL182W and YPL231W). It is the model’s lumped FAS reaction r_2140 scaled to six malonyl-CoA rounds, with release of the free acid:

acetyl-CoA + 6 malonyl-CoA + 12 NADPH + 17 H⁺ → myristate + 6 CO₂ + 7 CoA + 12 NADP⁺ + 5 H₂O

Free cytosolic myristate leaves through the existing transport reaction r_1777 and exchange r_2193. The reaction releases C14 completely and without limit, whereas V1254W in Yarrowia gave C14 as 11.6 % of fatty acids; no partial-release fraction (for example 10 %) was modelled, so the effect shown here is an upper bound on what a partial release could give. The SBML file with the reaction is generated by the team’s code and is held in the team’s project records; the equation above is complete.

Effect of the early-release reaction

In brief

With the new reaction switched on, all of the model’s C14 comes from it rather than from breaking down longer fatty acids. Maximum C14 export increased by 2.6 % at half of wild-type growth. Supplying more NADPH would raise it by 150.9 %.

Result. With the reaction active, 100 % of C14:0 originated from the added reaction and none from β-oxidation. Maximum C14:0 export increased by 2.6 % at 50 % of wild-type growth (0.119191 to 0.122237 mmol gDW⁻¹ h⁻¹; +3.1 % at 10 % growth and +2.6 % at 90 %). The sensitivity of C14 export to the availability of individual resources is shown in Figure 1; Figure A summarises the reaction and its effect.

Origin of the small effect

In brief

In the model, the early-release route saves little compared with the route that already existed. The real limits are the glucose supply and the NADPH needed to build the chain.

Direct formation of free C14:0 from FAS saved cofactors relative to the β-oxidation route already present in the model, and the saving was small once the credits of that route were included. Neither acetyl-CoA nor malonyl-CoA was limiting. The limits were glucose carbon (about 0.52 of the 1.0 mmol gDW⁻¹ h⁻¹ glucose uptake was committed to growth and maintenance at 50 % growth) and NADPH (12 per C14), which is produced in the oxidative pentose phosphate pathway at a carbon cost; ATP was second. With NADPH and ATP freely regenerated and no maintenance, the model reached the carbon-conservation limit of 0.42857 mmol gDW⁻¹ h⁻¹ (1/2.333), so in this model at this uptake the gap between this limit and the achieved yield is attributable to cofactor cost. This is a one-factor-at-a-time result.

The carbon budget is not fixed when a cofactor is freed. Without any C14 export, 50 % of wild-type growth needs 0.518 mmol gDW⁻¹ h⁻¹ of glucose; with NADPH freely regenerated it needs 0.284 (recomputed on 17 October 2026), because NADPH for biomass is also produced from glucose. This is why freeing NADPH raises C14 export from 0.122 to 0.307, above the 0.21 that the remaining 0.48 of glucose would allow at the carbon limit. Freeing NADPH or ATP is a diagnostic and not an engineering lever.

Limitations and model gaps

In brief

The added reaction is a modelling representation of what V1254W does, and the later knockout screen was run without it. Several genes were also missing or named differently in the model.

Caveat. The reaction represents the effect of V1254W and inherits assumption A1, which the wet-lab construct tests. The frontier and the original knockout screen of Step 4 were run on the model without the reaction. On 17 October 2026 the 175-set knockout screen was repeated with the reaction on (Step 4, Table 3); the frontier, the route analysis and the single-deletion screen were not.

Gaps and naming in Yeast9 identified during the work (from the team’s model comparison). Yeast9 was compared with the older S. cerevisiae model iMM904 (Yeast8 was not compared in the files available): at 50 % growth Yeast9 has open export reactions for C8:0, C10:0, C12:0, C14:0, C16:0, C16:1, C18:0 and C18:1, whereas iMM904 has an open export for C14:0 only (C10:0, C12:0, C16:0, C16:1, C18:0 and C18:1 exports exist but carry no flux; C8:0 has none). The following genes were flagged as absent: PEX11 (β-oxidation), ATR1 and CYP94C1 (ω-oxidation). FOX1 is present as POX1, a naming mismatch for the same function. It is expected that omission of the two ω-oxidation genes has little effect on yield; adding the missing reactions to the model and repeating the frontier would test this.
