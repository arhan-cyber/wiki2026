§2$409–445M market

Why did this market open, and why now?

Two clocks. The supplier left at the end of 2025; the regulator arrives around 2029.

Two clocks opened this market: the supplier left, and the regulator moved in behind it. Everything after this section is about the window between them.

In brief

Two clocks opened this market. 3M announced its exit from PFAS manufacturing in December 2022 and ceased making Novec and Fluorinert at the end of 2025. The EU's universal PFAS restriction, filed in January 2023 and backed by ECHA's committees in March 2026, could bind the remaining fluorinated substitutes from around 2029 — and there is no fluorinated escape route, because the substitutes sit inside the same class-based scope. The dielectric fluid market is worth $409–445M in 2026. India holds 3–4% of global data centre capacity and Mumbai 34–53% of India's, giving a serviceable market of roughly $4–8M.

2.1 How fast is the load growing?

Data centres consumed roughly 415 TWh of electricity in 2024 — about 1.5% of world consumption. The International Energy Agency's Base Case has predicted that this will more than double to around 945 TWh by 2030, just under 3% of global electricity, and reach approximately 1,200 TWh by 2035 6. The IEA's updated 2026 projection puts 2025 at 485 TWh and holds 2030 near 950 TWh 7. Consumption has grown about 12% a year since 2017, more than four times the rate of electricity demand overall 6.

The growth is concentrated. Electricity use by accelerated servers — the hardware running AI workloads — is projected to grow around 30% a year, against 9% for conventional servers 8.

2.2 Why can't air cooling keep up?

The binding constraint is not total energy but heat density per rack. Rack power is climbing past 30 kW against a traditional 5–10 kW 9, and immersion deployments are being specified for densities above 100 kW 10. Beyond roughly 30 kW, moving enough air through a rack stops being practical and liquid becomes an architectural requirement rather than an efficiency upgrade.

Cooling's share of facility electricity varies widely with design. The IEA's equipment breakdown puts cooling and environmental control at about 7% in efficient hyperscale sites and above 30% in less efficient enterprise sites 11. The opportunity sits at the inefficient end of that spread, and in the new high-density builds that would otherwise land there.

2.3 What actually opened this market?

The fluid market did not open gradually. It opened because the dominant supplier left and the regulator moved in behind it.

The supply clock. On 20 December 2022, 3M announced it would exit all PFAS manufacturing by the end of 2025 — explicitly including fluorinated fluids 2. The last date to place Novec orders was 31 March 2025 12, and manufacture ceased at the end of 2025 3. The withdrawn products — the Novec 7000 series, Novec 649, Fluorinert — were the working fluids on which two-phase immersion cooling was built.

The regulatory clock. On 13 January 2023, authorities in Denmark, Germany, the Netherlands, Norway and Sweden filed a universal PFAS restriction proposal under EU REACH 13. It defines PFAS structurally: any substance containing at least one fully fluorinated methyl or methylene carbon with no hydrogen, chlorine, bromine or iodine attached 14. On 2 March 2026 ECHA's Risk Assessment Committee adopted a final opinion supporting an EU-wide restriction 13. Its Socio-Economic Analysis Committee agreed on a draft opinion on 10 March 15 and opened a 60-day consultation that closed on 25 May 2026 13. The final opinion is due by the end of 2026 16, after which the Commission drafts the restriction text. Products could face severe restriction from around 2029 17.

Why this matters to a fluid supplier. The substitutes for Novec — Galden, Chemours and Honeywell grades — are themselves PFAS and sit inside the same class-based scope 18. There is no fluorinated escape route. Two independent analyst houses record the consequence directly: Mordor notes that mineral oil still leads fluid demand on price but bio-based and synthetic hydrocarbon alternatives are gaining traction under European PFAS restrictions 10; Persistence identifies bio-based dielectric fluids as the fastest-growing fluid category, driven by PFAS phase-out pressure 19.

2.5 Why Mumbai?

India's operational data centre stock reached approximately 1.5–1.7 GW of IT load in 2025 4,24,25, having added a record 387–440 MW in that year — roughly double 2024 24,25. Roughly 500 MW of further supply is expected in 2026, about 30% year-on-year growth 26. India holds only 3–4% of global data centre capacity while generating close to 20% of the world's data 24.

Within India, capacity is concentrated. Mumbai accounts for 34–53% of operational supply depending on source and definition 4,5,27, and Maharashtra holds 25–45% of upcoming capacity 28,29.

Applying India's 3–4% share of global capacity to the $409M fluid TAM gives a SAM of roughly $12–16M in 2026, and Mumbai's share of that a SOM of roughly $4–8M. These are small numbers, and they are the ones we plan against: built from the fluid market rather than the systems market, and from published capacity shares rather than a growth assumption of our own.

3.5 How does a fluid decision actually get made?

Fluid selection is not a purchasing decision made in isolation. It is made at the facility design stage, alongside tank and rack architecture, and it is constrained by hardware vendor compatibility. Industry sources identify limited standardisation and inconsistent compatibility between hardware vendors and immersion systems as a principal adoption barrier 37. Operators evaluate on Power Usage Effectiveness, Water Usage Effectiveness and Carbon Usage Effectiveness, with Indian industry leaders reporting PUE below 1.5 26.

The practical consequence for us: we must be qualified before a facility is designed, not after. That points at the 2027–2030 build wave, and at winning system manufacturers before operators.

§416 parameters

What does our fluid have to pass?

Sixteen parameters published by the Open Compute Project, two of which are legal gates rather than preferences.

The buyer does not evaluate our chemistry; they evaluate it against a published specification. That specification is the commercial gate, so we treat it as this venture's terms of trade rather than as a technical appendix.

In brief

The Open Compute Project publishes a base specification for immersion fluids covering sixteen parameters across six domains: thermal figures of merit, physical properties, electrical and signal integrity, chemical stability, safety, and environmental status. A companion document defines material compatibility testing against a three-band verdict. Two thresholds are legal gates rather than engineering preferences — the flash point requirement is tied to IEC 62368-1, which conditions market access for IT equipment in most jurisdictions. We treat the specification as this venture's terms of trade.

4.1 Why is there a published standard at all?

Immersion cooling only works if a fluid satisfies several requirements at once that pull against one another. It must conduct heat well and electricity not at all; it must be thin enough to pump cheaply and stable enough to sit against hot copper for a decade; it must not attack the seals, insulation or gaskets it is permanently in contact with.

Because these trade-offs are hard, the industry publishes a common specification rather than leaving each operator to invent one. The Open Compute Project Base Specification for Immersion Fluids 38, with its companion document on material compatibility 39, is the reference we assess ourselves against.

4.3 What must the fluid sit against for a decade?

A fluid sits in contact with copper, solder, polymers, elastomers and wire insulation for years. The companion specification defines the interaction mechanisms to be tested — dissolution, swelling, extraction of plasticisers, embrittlement, corrosion — and an accelerated ageing protocol with defined pass, case-by-case and fail bands 39.

4.4 Why is a specification a commercial document?

Two of the thresholds above are not engineering preferences but legal gates. The flash point requirement is tied to IEC 62368-1, compliance with which conditions market access for information technology equipment in most jurisdictions. A fluid that misses it cannot simply be sold to a willing buyer, however much that buyer wants it.

This is what connects §3 to §5. The buying sequence in §3.5 puts the fluid decision at facility design, inside a qualification conversation run by an engineer holding this document. Our scorecard is therefore not a self-assessment we chose to publish; it is the form in which the decision actually gets made. The gap between a specification line and a purchase order is the whole of the commercial problem, and it is why we describe the sixteen parameters that follow as terms of trade rather than as laboratory results.

It is also why the qualification premium in §7.5 exists at all. Crossing this specification is expensive and slow, which is precisely what makes the position on the other side of it worth holding.

5.3 Where do we lead?

Our thermal case is strong and it is the reason this molecule is worth pursuing at all. FOM1 of 65.36 clears the preferred Tier 2 threshold by 45%, and FOM2 clears its minimum by 80%. Viscosity passes with roughly fourfold margin, which matters commercially: pumping power is a recurring operating cost, and a thinner fluid at equal thermal performance is a cheaper fluid to run.

We are also PFAS-free by construction. There is no fluorine anywhere in the molecule, so no derogation, transition period or reformulation applies to us.

5.4 Which two specifications are still open?

Fourteen of the sixteen parameters are settled. Two remain open against the published standard, and both have a defined route to closure.

Open · Pour point

Methyl myristate solidifies at approximately 18–19 °C 44,45, against a specification of below −30 °C — a threshold set to guarantee mobility during unheated transport and in outdoor or edge deployments.

Inside an operating tank this is not a functional constraint, because the tank runs far above 18 °C. It binds on storage, transport and cold start, and it is disqualifying in cold-climate deployment without heat tracing. It is a principal reason we selected a tropical beachhead, where the property and the market are matched rather than in tension.

Open · Flash point

Closed-cup flash point is approximately 110–112 °C 41,47 against a requirement of ≥ 155 °C, tied to IEC 62368-1 38. This is the harder of the two, because the threshold conditions market access rather than merely engineering acceptance, and because no additive raises it (§5.7). It is therefore the governing constraint on formulation, and the study below is designed around it.

How both close

Both properties are governed by the same variable — carbon chain length — and move in opposite directions along it. The C12–C18 study below tests whether any single ester or blend clears both bounds at once. It is desk work on published data, needs no lab time and no funding, and returns one of two results we would publish either way: a formulation that clears the specification, or a documented demonstration that the saturated methyl ester series cannot, which is itself a finding worth recording. Fire-safety review is a planned stage of any qualification cycle and we are designing toward it rather than around it.

A structural observation. Across the fatty acid methyl ester series, flash point and melting point both rise with chain length. Longer chains fix the flash point and worsen the pour point; shorter chains do the reverse. C14 sits where it currently satisfies neither bound. Mapping this trade-off across C12–C18, and testing whether any single ester or blend clears both limits, is a purely computational piece of work and one of our highest-value next steps.

In progress · chain-length trade-off study

Mapping both bounds across the C12–C18 series

We are plotting flash point and melting point against carbon number for the saturated methyl ester series — methyl laurate (C12), our own C14, methyl palmitate (C16) and methyl stearate (C18) — with both OCP limits drawn as horizontal rules. Values come from PubChem, the NIST Chemistry WebBook and supplier safety data sheets. If a window exists where both clear, an open problem becomes a formulation decision. If none exists, a documented demonstration that the pure saturated series cannot satisfy both bounds is itself a result, and it redirects the work toward blends and additives with the reason on the record.

One caution we are carrying into the analysis. The two properties do not behave the same way in a mixture. Melting point depression means a blend melts below either component, so a mixture may clear the pour point when neither pure ester does. Flash point works the other way: a blend flashes near its most volatile component rather than the average. The chart cannot be read as though both properties average, and our conclusions will state which mechanism is doing the work.

5.5 When do the electrical measurements land?

Four parameters — dielectric strength, volume resistivity, dielectric constant and loss tangent — are the ones that define a dielectric fluid, and they are the immediate object of Stage 1 in §8.2. We set out what we expect, why, and what would change our mind.

What the chemistry predicts. Methyl myristate is a saturated, non-polar, fully covalent ester with no ionic content and no aromatic character. Volume resistivity and dielectric strength in fluids of this class are governed principally by ionic and moisture contamination rather than by the backbone itself, which is why purified esters are already an established immersion fluid class alongside synthetic hydrocarbons and fluorochemicals 40. Dielectric constant scales with molecular polarity; an ester group on a C14 chain gives a low dipole density, and published ester-based coolants sit inside the OCP ceiling of 2.3. Our expectation is that a ≥95% pure material clears all four.

Why expectation is not the deliverable. The values are sensitive to exactly the contaminants a real service life introduces — dissolved water, and the free fatty acid produced when an ester meets it (§5.6). A dry, freshly purified sample measured once would very likely pass and would tell a qualification engineer almost nothing. The measurement that counts is taken across the moisture range, on aged material.

How this closes

Stage 1 of §8.2, within this season. All four parameters are measurable on standard dielectric test equipment available to us through institutional facilities, on the neat fluid and again at the water-saturation limit. No biology gates this work and no external funding is required to begin it. A result outside the OCP band on any of the four would be a material finding, and we would publish it in the same terms as §5.4.

5.6 Does it stay stable over a service life?

As an ester, our fluid hydrolyses in the presence of trace moisture:

Both products matter. Liberated myristic acid raises total acid number against a ceiling of 0.03 mg KOH/g unused and 0.06 over lifetime. Methanol is volatile, toxic and odorous, against an odour ceiling of 200 ppb n-butanol equivalent sustained across a ten-year service life 38. Characterising this under accelerated ageing is a required, not optional, part of our programme.

5.7 What are we doing about it?

Hydrophobins

Amphiphilic fungal proteins that self-assemble at phase boundaries, reducing surface tension and interfacial viscosity, suppressing bubble formation at heated surfaces and promoting uniform boundary-layer flow.

Butylated hydroxyanisole (BHA)

A lipid-soluble radical scavenger. Under sustained thermal load, ester degradation generates free radicals that propagate a chain reaction producing volatile, low-boiling breakdown products. BHA intercepts the radicals and arrests the cascade.

Tocopherols

Natural fat-soluble antioxidants serving a dual role: an alternative radical scavenger, and a compatibility buffer moderating the fluid's solvent action on wire insulation.

What these additives do and do not do

The antioxidants protect the flash point against degradation-driven decline across the service life. They do not raise the intrinsic flash point of the neat compound from 112 °C to 155 °C, because flash point is governed by the vapour pressure of the volatile fraction present. Closing that gap remains an open problem and we state it as one.

5.8 What else did we consider?

Calmodulin, assessed as a responsive structural stabiliser, was rejected on biophysical grounds: its size and anisotropic charge distribution give an unfavourable dielectric constant and high polarisation under operational fields, introducing capacitive loss rather than insulation.

Methyl oleate, an intuitive blend candidate, was rejected for unsaturation. Its C=C double bond is susceptible to oxidation, polymerisation and cross-linking under thermal load, producing viscosity rise and efficiency loss.

6.3 Why ferment a molecule that already exists cheaply?

The question we expect first

Why ferment a molecule the oleochemical industry already makes cheaply?

Methyl myristate is produced conventionally by esterifying methanol with myristic acid, the acid obtained by fractionating coconut or palm kernel oil. It is sold in bulk by Wilmar International, and manufactured in Ambernath, Mumbai by Mubychem — inside our own beachhead market. We did not discover this late; it is the central commercial question about this project.

Our answer has three parts. Two are structural advantages we hold today. The third is a cost position that improves with process maturity, and we set out its current state rather than its best case.

First, we are not selling a molecule. We are selling a qualified dielectric fluid. Bulk fatty acids trade at roughly $2–4/kg. Engineered dielectric fluids sell many multiples above that. The spread reflects specification, documentation, qualification and warranty — not chemistry. Any entrant, oleochemical or fermentative, must cross it, and crossing it is most of the work.

Second, feedstock decoupling. The oleochemical route is structurally tied to coconut and palm kernel oil, carrying land-use, deforestation and seasonal price exposure. Our pathway is designed to accept variable crude carbon streams including biodiesel by-product glycerol.

Third, cost. At present titres fermentation does not beat esterification on price, and we do not build the case on price. §7 states the gap numerically and shows why the case does not need it: the value sits in the qualification premium, not in the molecule.

7.4 What would a fermentation route cost?

We do not have to guess. Peer-reviewed techno-economic analyses exist for microbial production of comparable hydrophobic, secreted, broth-extracted molecules.

The closest published analogue is a full plant-scale techno-economic analysis of microbial limonene production. It reports a minimum selling price of $19.9/kg at a fermentation productivity of 0.7 kg/(m³·h), achieved at 45% of maximum theoretical yield. Raising productivity from 0.2 to 1.0 kg/(m³·h) lowers minimum selling price by 70%, to $16.29/kg — implying roughly $54/kg at the low end of that range. The analysis identifies product yield, substrate price, capital investment, project length and hurdle rate as the dominant cost drivers 53.

A second analysis, on industrial-scale enzyme fermentation, isolates downstream processing as the single largest lever: the same product costs approximately $2,300/kg as a crude preparation and $99,000/kg purified, a fortyfold difference driven by purification strategy alone 54. Broader review work confirms that yield, titre and productivity improvements act on minimum selling price through distinct and non-interchangeable mechanisms 55.

What this implies for us. A mature fermentation route to a molecule of this class plausibly reaches $16–20/kg at good productivity, and considerably more below it. Our own downstream case rests on extracellular secretion avoiding cell lysis — which, on the evidence of 54, is where the cost is won or lost.

7.5 Does the arithmetic work?

Two conclusions follow, and we state both.

First, fermentation does not beat esterification on cost for this molecule. A four- to eightfold structural cost gap is not closed by optimisation. This venture does not win on price and is not built to.

Second, that may not matter. If qualified dielectric fluid genuinely clears $50/kg, a $16–20/kg fermentation route retains substantial margin. The venture's viability therefore rests entirely on whether the qualified-fluid premium is real — which is precisely the number §7.3 says we cannot yet verify. This is the most important open commercial question on this page.

7.6 Is price per litre even the right measure?

Price per litre is not the operator's decision variable; total cost of ownership is. Immersion cooling reduces water consumption sharply against evaporative cooling — a material operating cost in water-stressed Mumbai, and one that never appears in a per-litre comparison. Reported fluid service life varies widely across sources, and we regard establishing our own figure through accelerated aging as a priority rather than citing someone else's.

7.7 Why publish no revenue forecast?

We publish the inputs a revenue model requires rather than a model built without them. Each is a defined piece of work with a known route to an answer:

  1. A verified market price per litre for qualified dielectric fluid (§7.3)
  2. A demonstrated titre for C14 ester secretion in S. cerevisiae — published work reaches 674 mg/L for this ester class, with product distribution favouring C16 and C18 49,50
  3. Achievable downstream recovery, which 54 indicates dominates cost
  4. A qualification timeline, which governs when revenue could begin
  5. Licensing royalty terms, which determine what share of value accrues to us

A seven-year forecast built without these would be arithmetic, not information.

9.2 Is a fermented product regulated as a GMO?

A point we want to be precise about, because it is commercially significant and frequently confused.

The organism. Our engineered S. cerevisiae is a genetically modified organism and falls under India's institutional biosafety framework — Institutional Biosafety Committee review, and RCGM and GEAC approvals at scale.

The product. The commercial article is a purified, non-living chemical ester. It contains no genetic material and is regulated as an industrial chemical: EPA TSCA inventory listing in the United States, REACH registration in the European Union, and applicable BIS and CPCB requirements in India.

These are genuinely separate routes. The product-side pathway is a conventional chemical registration rather than a GMO approval, which materially shortens time to market relative to a living-product biotechnology.

9.3 Which thresholds gate market access?

Two thresholds gate market access rather than merely engineering acceptance. IEC 62368-1 conditions the sale of information technology equipment in most jurisdictions and is the basis for the flash point requirement we currently fail 38. The EU PFAS restriction (§2.3) does not constrain us, but it constrains a competitor class, and its final scope will be determined by the SEAC opinion due at the end of 2026 16.

9.4 Why no patents?

We have deferred patent protection to spin-out, and set out the reasoning here.

iGEM requires full public disclosure on the team wiki. India and most jurisdictions apply absolute novelty, so disclosure forecloses patentability of what is disclosed. We chose disclosure.

The cost of that choice is lower than it first appears. Methyl myristate is a known compound produced industrially for decades, so composition-of-matter protection was never available to anyone. What could have been protected is our construct architecture — and at proof of concept, before a commercially relevant titre, a claim over a construct we cannot yet run at scale protects the least valuable part of the asset.

The defensible value sits in three things this wiki does not disclose: the working strain as a physical transferable asset; process know-how held as trade secret; and the qualification dataset, which is slow and expensive to reproduce and is not a patent question at all. This is consistent with the licensing model in §8.4 — we would transfer a strain and a validated process, not assert a claim.

Process claims, formulation claims and claims over improved strains all remain available at spin-out, which is the point at which they carry value. Investors in synthetic biology frequently require exclusivity, so this is a decision to be revisited then rather than a door closed now. We have deferred protection, not forgone it.
